Insights · I — On Decisions

The Fifteen-Year Horizon

Why most families approach cross-border education decisions one stage at a time — and why that framing systematically underestimates what is actually being chosen.

Most families approach cross-border education decisions one stage at a time: which school for primary, which programme for secondary, which university afterwards. The decisions feel like a sequence. They are not.

A family that places a seven-year-old in Switzerland, Luxembourg or the Netherlands thinks they are choosing a school. What they are actually choosing is the order of languages their child will think in fifteen years from now, the nationality composition of their child's social network, the range of professional pathways that remain open, and — for many families — the legal system in which their child will eventually come of age. The compounding effects are large; the reversibility narrows quickly.

The biggest cost does not appear on tuition statements. It appears as this: a wrong early decision requires fifteen years of detour to repair — usually at the cost of the child's language fluency, professional direction, or sense of self. This cost is invisible at the moment of decision, which is why families systematically underestimate it.

This reframing changes which questions matter. The ranking of a school becomes less important than the trajectory it opens. The fee of a programme becomes less important than the costs avoided by sequencing decisions correctly. The reputation of a country becomes less important than the structural fit between that country's system and the family's actual long-term intentions.

Good advisory work is not about recommending schools. It is about helping a family see the fifteen-year shape of what they are choosing — and seeing it while there is still time to change it.
